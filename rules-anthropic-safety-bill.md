# Anthropic's draft AI safety bill, in plain English

*From my "The AI safety company just wrote its own safety law" short. — Dara*

Anthropic, the AI lab best known for talking about safety, has been privately circulating a draft bill it wants Congress to adopt. Here's what's in it, what it would actually do, and why I called it a lock instead of a brake.

Source: [TechCrunch, Oct 20, 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/20/anthropic-pitches-ai-safety-bill-that-may-block-a-model-if-50-people-could-be-killed/)

---

## What the bill says

**1. The government could suspend an AI model, but only in extreme cases.**
There has to be a *foreseeable* risk of:
- **50 or more deaths**, or
- **$1 billion or more in damage**

…and the risk has to come from one of three things:
- the model helping build a **bioweapon**
- the model **evading its own controls**
- a **major cyberattack**

**2. Only one door to stop a model.**
Only the **Commerce Department** can act, and even then it can't pause a model itself. It has to **ask a federal judge**, and the judge has to agree.

**3. Everyone else is locked out.**
**Every other federal agency is barred from blocking a model's release.**

**4. It's not law, or even close.**
It has **no sponsor** in Congress and **no committee action** yet. It's Anthropic's opening bid: a draft of the rules before Congress writes its own.

---

## Why I called it a lock, not a brake

On paper this is a safety mechanism: there's a way to stop a dangerous model. But read who gets to use it:

| On paper | In practice |
|---|---|
| "The government can pause a dangerous model" | Only at 50+ deaths or $1B+ damage, from three specific threats |
| "There's oversight" | One department can ask; a judge decides |
| "Regulators are involved" | Every other agency is explicitly *barred* from blocking a release |

A brake is something other people can press. This sets a very high bar, names exactly one person allowed to reach for it, and removes everyone else's hands from the wheel. That's the tension: **the company being regulated is drafting the limits on who can regulate it.**

## The fair counterpoint

To be balanced, there's a real argument for it: a single, clear process is easier to follow than dozens of agencies each able to block a release for different reasons, and it puts a judge (not a company) in the final seat. Reasonable people disagree on where the bar should sit. My point is narrower: **read who writes the rules, not just what they promise.**

## What to watch next

- Does any member of Congress **sponsor** it?
- Do the **thresholds** (50 deaths / $1B) change in any version that moves forward?
- Do other labs back it, or push their own drafts?
